Open source · historical astronomy · audio

Xuanji璇玑

A Python library that turns a light curve or a dated sky-record into sound. It maps the numbers you already have to pitch, tempo, and stereo position by fixed rules, so one record always returns the same audio. It does not compose, and it does not fill gaps. The tool interprets the data; it does not author it.

What the library is for

01

It reads a record and returns audio, deterministically

Give it a light curve, an eclipse timing, or a night-by-night comet track. The same input always produces the same waveform. No model guesses at missing nights, and nothing is invented between the data points.

02

It listens to change that a plot flattens

A supernova fading over two years, a totality that arrives as silence, a dark grain crossing the Sun: these read as slow curves on a page. As pitch and tempo they are easier to compare across events, and the ear catches a break the eye skims past.

03

Every event is cross-identified, with the uncertainty kept

Each curated record names its primary source (a court chronicle, a treatise), its modern cross-identification, and how firm that link is. A debated remnant stays labelled debated. The sibling archive supplies the records; the library is their playback engine.

04

It is small, tested, and cite-able

Three mapping rules, a light-curve type, a sky-path type, and a plotter. Pinned dependencies, a 214-test suite, a Zenodo deposit, and a peer-reviewed paper in the Journal of Open Source Software. You can read the whole thing in an afternoon.

Featured record · XJ-1054

The guest star of 1054, made audible

Song-dynasty court astronomers logged a 客星 (guest star) near Tianguan on 4 July 1054. It stayed visible in daylight for about 23 days and at night for roughly two years. Modern astronomy cross-identifies that record with the Crab Nebula and its pulsar, in Taurus.

Xuanji reconstructs the decline as a light curve and maps it to a falling pitch. What you hear is not a tune. It is the record itself, resampled into a sense the record was never written for.

The three rules

How a record becomes sound

Three fixed, documented mappings do all the work. There are no learned parameters and no randomness. Read the full model on the Mapping page.

brightnessapparent magnitude
pitchbrighter reads higher
timedays / nights / months
temporecord time to playback
sky positionazimuth / declination
stereowhere it stood in the sky
deterministic · no learned weights · same record in, same audio out

Built on the scientific-Python stack

Astropy
coordinates, precession, and time scales, so a record's position is placed in the sky of its own date.
NumPy · SciPy
the light-curve arrays and the resampling that turns record-time into audio-rate samples.
soundfile
writes WAV and FLAC. Playback and figures use only the returned array, nothing hidden.
Xuanji Archive
the sibling project that supplies the curated, cross-identified records this engine plays.

Adoption

Where Xuanji is used

The library reaches beyond the archive it was built for. Two audiences drive most of the downloads: educators who want a record to be heard as well as read, and people who read the sky by ear because they cannot read it by eye.

Classrooms
history-of-science and astronomy seminars that assign a guest-star record and its sonification side by side, so students hear where the data thins out.
Planetaria
dark-sky and public-astronomy programs that play a reconstructed decline under a projected sky, pairing the recovered record with the light-polluted present.
Accessibility
screen-reader astronomy curricula that use the deterministic mapping so a blind learner and a sighted learner discuss the same audio of the same curve.
Research notes
cited in methods sections where authors sonify a reconstructed light curve to communicate the shape and the uncertainty of a coarse historical record.

Roughly 4,900 downloads a month from PyPI and conda-forge; 61 dependent notebooks on public GitHub. See the Cite page for works that reference the library.

Maintainers & contributors

Who builds Xuanji

Xuanji began as one student's engine for a sky-records archive and is now maintained by a small volunteer team, most of them secondary-school and early-undergraduate contributors who added a record, a mapping test, or a translation. Contribution is open; see CONTRIBUTING.

Peer-reviewed · JOSS

The paper

Xuanji is described in a short, peer-reviewed paper in the Journal of Open Source Software. It documents the mapping model, the determinism guarantee, and the reconstruction-and-uncertainty method that lets a text record be treated as a light curve.

Yeoh, A., Okonkwo, M., Ramanathan, D., Halvorsen, S., Meng, L., & Balasubramanian, P. (2026). Xuanji: sonification of historical astronomical records. Journal of Open Source Software, 11(118), 6842.
