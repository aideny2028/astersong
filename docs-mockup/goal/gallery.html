Guides / Audio gallery

The audio gallery

Forty-two curated records, each rendered by the library with the mapping it was published with. Ten are laid out in full below, with the plate, the player, and the record card. The remaining thirty-two are listed in the catalog; every one of them regenerates locally from xj.records(). Nothing here is a tune. Each sound is a reading of the numbers a record already holds.

Every card names four things: the primary source that recorded the event, the modern cross-identification that ties the old text to a known object, the confidence of that link, and the uncertainty the sound is allowed to carry. A debated remnant stays labelled debated, and the audio never claims more precision than the record holds.

cross-id
the modern object a historical record is matched to, by position, date, and described behaviour.
confidence
secure · well-attested · computed · candidate · debated. It grades the match, not the record.
uncertainty
folded into the sound through date_sigma and pos_sigma, so a coarse record sounds coarse.
Pitch grid Notes fall on 12-tone equal temperament, the tuning whose complete mathematical derivation Zhu Zaiyu first published in 1584. Temperament is a listening convenience only; it carries no data and can be turned off with grid="continuous".
XJ-1054 · sonification22.4 s
XJ-1054 · supernova

The guest star of 1054

Song-dynasty court astronomers logged a 客星 near Tianguan on 4 July 1054. It stayed visible in daylight about 23 days and at night roughly two years. The declining light is mapped to a falling pitch.

  • sourceSong Shi, Astronomical Treatise; Song Huiyao
  • cross-idCrab Nebula (M1), Taurus
  • confidencesecure
  • uncertaintydate to the day; light curve reconstructed from brightness phrases, so magnitudes carry roughly +/-0.5 mag
  • mappingbrightness → pitch (log flux, 220-1760 Hz); ~2 yr → 22 s
XJ-1006 · sonification25.5 s
XJ-1006 · supernova

The brightest recorded stellar event

Recorded across Song China, the Islamic world, Europe, and Japan in the spring of 1006. Ali ibn Ridwan in Cairo compared it to a quarter Moon. Its extreme peak reads as the highest, most sustained pitch in the gallery.

  • sourceSong Shi; Ali ibn Ridwan, commentary on the Tetrabiblos; St Gallen annals
  • cross-idSN 1006 remnant (PKS 1459-41), Lupus
  • confidencesecure
  • uncertaintypeak magnitude debated (about -7.5 mag); onset placed to within a few days by cross-source agreement
  • mappingbrightness → pitch; multi-source spread → stereo width via pos_sigma
XJ-0185 · sonification26.7 s
XJ-0185 · supernova, debated

The guest star of 185

The Book of Later Han records a guest star between Nan Men (near Alpha and Beta Centauri) on 7 December 185 CE, fading over months. Whether the text describes a supernova or a comet is still argued, and the sound keeps that doubt audible with a wide onset jitter.

  • sourceHou Hanshu (Book of Later Han), Treatise on Astronomy
  • cross-idRCW 86 (candidate remnant)
  • confidencecandidate
  • uncertaintySN-versus-comet reading unresolved; duration phrase ("some months") sets the light curve, not measured magnitudes
  • mappingbrightness → pitch; jitter="date_sigma" softens every onset to mark the coarseness
XJ-1604 · sonification23.9 s
XJ-1604 · supernova

Kepler's supernova, 1604

The last naked-eye supernova seen in the Milky Way, watched in October 1604 in Europe, China, and Korea. Its European, Chinese, and Korean light estimates are reconstructed into one curve, and the small disagreements between them widen the tone rather than being smoothed away.

  • sourceKepler, De Stella Nova (1606); Ming Shilu; Korean Seonjo Sillok
  • cross-idKepler's SNR (SN 1604), Ophiuchus
  • confidencesecure
  • uncertaintycross-source magnitude scatter of ~0.6 mag carried into stereo width
  • mappingbrightness → pitch; three-source scatter → pos_sigma stereo
XJ-1408 · sonification14.6 s
XJ-1408 · guest star, debated

The guest star of 1408

A Ming record notes a yellow guest star near the Tianjin asterism, in Cygnus, in the autumn of 1408. Modern readings range from an ordinary nova to a speculative tie with an X-ray source in the same field; the library renders only the record, and labels the identification debated.

  • sourceMing Shilu (Veritable Records of the Ming)
  • cross-idnova in Cygnus (debated; X-ray-source tie is speculative)
  • confidencedebated
  • uncertaintycolour phrase ("yellow") and short duration give a weak curve; identification unresolved and marked so
  • mappingbrightness → pitch; position → stereo (Cygnus, near Deneb)
XJ-CMT-1066 · sonification22.7 s
XJ-CMT-1066 · comet, sky-path

Halley's comet, the 1066 return

A SkyPath, not a light curve: the comet's night-by-night motion is recorded, so brightness sets pitch while the recorded position pans the tone across the stereo field. You hear it cross the sky. The 1066 apparition is the one stitched into the Bayeux Tapestry.

  • sourceSong Shi; European chronicles; Anglo-Saxon Chronicle
  • cross-id1P/Halley, 1066 apparition
  • confidencesecure (orbit back-computed)
  • uncertaintynightly positions carried as pos_sigma; brightness phrases coarse, so the pitch track is smoothed only within a night, never across a gap
  • mappingbrightness → pitch; sky motion → stereo pan (SkyPath)
XJ-ECL-0709BCE · sonification17.9 s
XJ-ECL-0709BCE · solar eclipse

The eclipse of 709 BCE, made silent

The Spring and Autumn Annals records "the Sun was eaten" on a day answering to 17 July 709 BCE. Brightness falls to a hush at totality: the mapping turns the deepest light into the deepest quiet, then lets the tone climb back as the disc reappears.

  • sourceChunqiu (Spring and Autumn Annals), Duke Huan year 3
  • cross-idtotal solar eclipse, 17 July 709 BCE (back-computed)
  • confidencecomputed
  • uncertaintyof 37 solar eclipses in the Annals, most match modern computation; the record fixes the date, ephemeris supplies the light curve
  • mappingbrightness → pitch, floored to silence at totality; gap="mark" ticks the contacts
XJ-SUN-0028BCE · sonification19.2 s
XJ-SUN-0028BCE · sunspot

A black vapor on the Sun, 28 BCE

The Book of Han reports a dark vapor "as large as a coin" at the center of the Sun on a day in 28 BCE, one of the earliest dated naked-eye sunspot records. Each recorded sighting becomes a low grain against the solar tone; the run of sightings sets the rhythm.

  • sourceHanshu (Book of Han), Treatise on the Five Elements
  • cross-idnaked-eye sunspot group (no cycle attribution)
  • confidencesecure record, cycle unknown
  • uncertaintysize phrase gives relative area only; solar-cycle placement impossible this far back and is left blank
  • mappingspot area → grain amplitude; sighting dates → tempo
XJ-MET-0036 · sonification14.6 s
XJ-MET-0036 · meteor storm, debated

"Stars fell like rain", 36 CE

The Book of Later Han records stars falling like rain before dawn in 36 CE. The shower that produced it is argued over, so the sonification maps only the counts the text gives: each meteor is a grain transient, and the density of grains is the storm.

  • sourceHou Hanshu (Book of Later Han)
  • cross-idradiant/shower undetermined (Perseid and Leonid readings both proposed)
  • confidencedebated
  • uncertaintyno rate count survives; grain density set from the qualitative phrase, so it is illustrative and labelled so
  • mappingmeteor count → grain density; pre-dawn window → tempo
XJ-CHART-DUNHUANG · sonification22.4 s
XJ-CHART-DUNHUANG · star chart

The Dunhuang star chart, scanned into sound

The oldest surviving manuscript star atlas, drawn about 700 CE and held by the British Library. Not a light curve at all: the mapping sweeps the chart in right ascension and voices each plotted star, its brightness setting pitch, its declination the stereo height. You hear the sky as the chart drew it.

  • sourceDunhuang star chart, ~700 CE (British Library, Or.8210/S.3326)
  • cross-idTang-era whole-sky atlas, ~1,300 stars in 257 asterisms
  • confidencewell-attested
  • uncertaintythe ~700 CE date spans a reign-length window; the chart is schematic, so plotted positions carry a wide pos_sigma
  • mappingright ascension → tempo (scan); star brightness → pitch; declination → stereo

The full catalog

The ten records above are the rendered set. The gallery holds forty-two in total; the remaining thirty-two are listed here with the same four fields. Each ships with the package and renders locally in one line, xj.sonify(xj.from_record(id)), so the audio is reproducible rather than pre-baked. The astronomy is fixed: SN 1006 in Lupus, SN 1054 in Taurus, SN 185 as the RCW 86 candidate, SN 1604 as Kepler's, the Suzhou planisphere engraved in 1247 CE.

Contribute a record

The gallery grows the way the sibling archive grows: one well-sourced record at a time, most of them added by students. A record is accepted when it carries a citable primary source, a stated cross-identification with an honest confidence grade, and the uncertainty that grade implies. You do not need to be an astronomer; you need to read a source carefully and refuse to overclaim.

  1. Read the contributing guide and the record schema.
  2. Draft the record: source citation, date (with date_sigma), position (with pos_sigma), cross-id, and confidence.
  3. Run the schema validator and the determinism check locally, then open a pull request. A maintainer reviews the cross-identification before it merges.
The one rule that never bends A record is only as certain as its source. If the identification is debated, it ships labelled debated, and the sound is given the jitter that doubt deserves. Xuanji would rather sound coarse than sound false.
