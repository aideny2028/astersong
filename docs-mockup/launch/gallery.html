Ten records, made audible

Each entry below is a historical astronomical record rendered by the three fixed mapping rules. Every one names its primary source, its modern cross-identification, and how firm that link is. Where the identification is only a candidate, the entry says so, and the sound carries that uncertainty rather than hiding it. These are the ten records that ship curated in v0.3.1.

Read each card the way you would read a catalogue entry, not a track listing. The figure is a two-panel plate from xj.plot(): the waveform above, a log-frequency spectrogram below. The player is the rendered .wav. The three-line data block is what makes the audio citable, and it is the point of the whole project: a note in a court chronicle only becomes usable evidence once you can say which modern object it names, and with what confidence.

Primary source
the record as written: a court chronicle, a treatise, a star atlas. Sonification never replaces reading the source; it is one more way to interrogate it.
Cross-ID & confidence
the modern object the record is matched to, and whether that match is secure or still a candidate. A debated remnant stays labelled debated.
Mapping
which rules were applied and why. Brightness to pitch, time to tempo, sky-position to stereo, with the record's uncertainty folded in.

plate · SN 1006 / Lupus. The brightest stellar event on record sits at the top of the pitch range.

SN 1006, the brightest of them all

XJ-1006 · supernova · Lupus

A guest star seen in spring 1006 was recorded independently across Eurasia. It is the brightest stellar event in the historical record, bright enough to read by. Four cultures logging one object is what makes its brightness history trustworthy.

  • SourceSong Shi and Song Hui Yao (China); Ali ibn Ridwan's commentary on Ptolemy (Egypt); Japanese and European annals
  • Cross-IDSNR G327.6+14.6 (PKS 1459-41), a Type Ia remnant
  • Confidencesecure
  • Mappingbrightness → pitch; peak magnitude near -7.5 pins the top note, then a slow multi-month decline
XJ-1006 · sonificationLupus

plate · SN 1054 / Taurus. ~2 years of visibility compressed to 22 s; onsets softened by date uncertainty.

The guest star of 1054, the Crab

XJ-1054 · supernova · Taurus

Song-dynasty astronomers logged a 客星 near Tianguan on 4 July 1054, visible in daylight for about 23 days and at night for roughly two years. The reconstruction is a hypothesis about its brightness history, drawn from dated phrases in the treatise.

  • SourceSong Shi, Astronomical Treatise; guest star near Tianguan (zeta Tauri)
  • Cross-IDCrab Nebula (M1) and its pulsar
  • Confidencesecure
  • Mappingbrightness → pitch, ~2 yr → 22 s; jitter="date_sigma" blurs uncertain onsets
XJ-1054 · sonificationTaurus

plate · SN 185 / near Nanmen. The wide, blurred onset is the debated cross-identification made audible.

The guest star of 185, a debated remnant

XJ-0185 · supernova · near Alpha / Beta Centauri

The Book of Later Han records a guest star of December 185 near Nanmen, visible for months. It is often called the earliest recorded supernova, but the link is not settled: some read the duration as too long for a supernova.

  • SourceHou Han Shu (Book of Later Han), Treatise on Astronomy; guest star near Nanmen
  • Cross-IDRCW 86 (candidate); a slow-nova reading has also been argued
  • Confidencecandidate · debated
  • Mappingbrightness → pitch; the coarse duration is left coarse, and the identity flag rides through to the sound
XJ-0185 · sonificationcandidate remnant

plate · SN 1604 / Ophiuchus. Reconstructed from Kepler's and Korean dated magnitude estimates.

Kepler's star of 1604

XJ-1604 · supernova · Ophiuchus

First seen on 9 October 1604 and studied by Johannes Kepler, who published a book on it. Korean and Chinese court records give it a densely dated brightness track, the last confirmed naked-eye supernova in our galaxy.

  • SourceKepler, De Stella Nova (1606); Korean Seonjo Sillok; Ming court records
  • Cross-IDKepler's SNR (G4.5+6.8)
  • Confidencesecure
  • Mappingbrightness → pitch from dated magnitude estimates; time → tempo over ~12 months
XJ-1604 · sonificationOphiuchus

plate · guest star of 1408 / Cygnus region. A short-lived event whose identity is still open.

The guest star of 1408

XJ-1408 · nova (candidate) · Cygnus region

Ming records note a yellow guest star in 1408 near Niandao, in the region of Cygnus. It is a short, well-dated event, but the modern identification is unsettled and some proposed associations are speculative. It is included precisely to show how the library handles an open case.

  • SourceMing Shilu (Veritable Records of the Ming) and local gazetteers; guest star near Niandao
  • Cross-IDunresolved; candidate nova in Cygnus, proposed X-ray-source links are not accepted
  • Confidencedebated · identity open
  • Mappingbrightness → pitch; a brief high onset that fades, its short baseline left as short as the record
XJ-1408 · sonificationcandidate nova

plate · Halley apparition. A dated night-by-night track: the pan carries the comet's motion across the sky.

A comet track: Halley across the sky

XJ-HALLEY · comet · moving object

Chinese court astronomers logged comets night by night with positions against the asterisms. Threaded across apparitions, those tracks belong to one periodic comet. This is the library's positional case: a SkyPath, not a light curve.

  • SourceChinese apparition records; the -240 sighting in the Shiji is the earliest generally accepted
  • Cross-ID1P/Halley, orbital period ~76 years
  • Confidencesecure
  • Mappingposition → stereo (the pan follows the recorded track); brightness → pitch
XJ-HALLEY · sonificationposition → stereo

plate · Dunhuang chart (~700 CE, British Library). A sonified map: a sweep across charted stars, not a time series.

The Dunhuang star chart, a sky read as sound

XJ-DUNHUANG · chart · whole-sky atlas

The Dunhuang manuscript is the oldest surviving portable star atlas, held by the British Library (Or.8210/S.3326) and dated to about 700 CE. It charts more than 1,300 stars. There is no light curve here; the sound is a traverse of the map itself.

  • SourceDunhuang star atlas, British Library Or.8210/S.3326, from the Mogao caves
  • Cross-IDits asterisms matched to the modern sky; the ~700 CE date is a scholarly estimate
  • Confidenceobject secure; dating ~700 CE estimated
  • Mappinga sky sweep: each charted star's position → stereo, brightness → pitch
XJ-DUNHUANG · sonificationmap traverse

plate · a dated solar eclipse. The tone falls to silence at totality, then returns.

A solar eclipse: totality as silence

XJ-ECLIPSE · eclipse · timed event

Chinese chronicles carry some of the oldest dated eclipse observations, kept because a failure of the Sun demanded a court response. Back-computed against modern ephemerides, a phrase becomes a timed light curve that drops to nothing at totality.

  • SourceChinese court eclipse records (Chunqiu-era annals and later chronicles)
  • Cross-IDmatched to a specific dated eclipse via modern ephemeris computation
  • Confidencesecure (once the date resolves to one eclipse)
  • Mappingbrightness → pitch; totality reads as silence; time → tempo across contact phases
XJ-ECLIPSE · sonificationtotality → silence

plate · a meteor storm. Each recorded meteor is a grain transient; the display rate sets the tempo.

A night the stars fell like rain

XJ-METEOR · meteor · timed display

Chinese observers logged nights when meteors fell too fast to count, sometimes with directions and rough rates. Linking one dated storm to a known stream is a hypothesis, so the association is kept as a candidate, not stated as fact.

  • SourceChinese records of dense meteor displays, logged with counts and directions
  • Cross-IDcandidate historical return of a known stream (e.g. an early Leonid outburst)
  • Confidencestream association candidate
  • Mappingeach meteor → a grain transient; recorded rate → tempo, so a storm is a rush of onsets
XJ-METEOR · sonificationrate → tempo

plate · a naked-eye sunspot. A dark grain in the Sun reads as a low dip in an otherwise steady tone.

A dark spot within the Sun

XJ-SUNSPOT · sunspot · solar record

The Book of Han describes a black vapour "as large as a coin" within the Sun in 28 BCE, among the earliest dated sunspot records, seen through haze at naked-eye scale. A run of such records tracks solar activity long before telescopes.

  • SourceHan Shu (Book of Han), 28 BCE; later Chinese naked-eye sunspot notes
  • Cross-IDa large naked-eye sunspot group, visible at high activity through haze
  • Confidencesecure as a sunspot record; individual dating varies
  • Mappingthe dark grain → a low dip in a bright steady tone; time → tempo across days visible
XJ-SUNSPOT · sonificationdark grain → dip

Contribute a record

The curated set is small on purpose: every record here was read against its primary source and given an explicit cross-identification and confidence before it shipped. If you have a dated observation with a source you can point to, it can join the set. A contribution is a record file, not a sound file: the audio is generated deterministically from the mapping.

What a record needs A primary source you can cite, a dated brightness or position series transcribed from it, a proposed cross-identification with an honest confidence label, and the coordinates in the record's own epoch. Debated is a valid confidence: an unresolved case is welcome as long as it is marked unresolved. See CONTRIBUTING.md.

An advisory slot for source review is currently open. Until it is filled, cross-identifications are carried at the confidence the published literature supports, and nothing is upgraded from candidate to secure without a citation.
